Abstract
The paper provides a review of publications devoted to the problem of satellite magnetic attitude control over the past five years. The results presented in the reviewed works are briefly described. Several examples are examined in detail, showcasing the use of theoretical mechanics methods to achieve practical results directly applicable to spacecraft control systems, and the combined utilization of an understanding of the system dynamic properties and modern computational methods.

Abstract
Particle swarm optimization (PSO) is a non-gradient biologically inspired global optimization method. It utilizes direct calculation of the optimized cost function during the numerical simulation of the process in question. Significantly complex and implicit functions that prohibit clear gradient expression are effectively handled by PSO. This feature is utilized in the paper to construct specific angular trajectory of a magnetically actuated satellite. A cost function is suggested that confines the control torque corresponding to this trajectory to the plane orthogonal to the geomagnetic induction vector. Optimal trajectory coefficients are found by PSO. The control structure is based on the Lyapunov feedback approach and explicitly accommodates the gravitational and aerodynamic torques influence. PSO procedure provides the optimal control gains on this stage. Stability of the resulting motion is demonstrated. Numerical simulation example with different disturbance factors illustrates the expected stabilization accuracy of the order of few degrees.

Introduction
Small spacecraft (SC) are widely applicable today. They can be used either alone or in a group flight, which allows them to solve a large range of tasks. Small SC are usually made simple to keep the cost and design complexity down, while maintaining high reliability. Therefore, a magnetic attitude control system is often used, since it has low energy consumption, it is small and easy to manufacture. More than a half of CubeSats utilize active magnetic attitude control, which goes up to almost three-quarter with passive magnetic control [1]. An important weak point is the absence of the control torque 𝐌𝑚⁢𝑎⁢𝑔⁡𝑛=𝐦×𝐁𝑚⁢𝑎⁢𝑔⁡𝑛 along the geomagnetic induction vector 𝐁𝑚⁢𝑎⁢𝑔⁡𝑛. However, the direction of vector 𝐁𝑚⁢𝑎⁢𝑔⁡𝑛 changes during the satellite motion along the orbit and all directions become available with time. The system is generally controllable [2,3].
The problem of providing SC stabilization using only three-axis magnetic attitude control system proposed in Refs. [4,5] is widely considered now. The main concept for the control construction is a Lyapunov feedback law. Feedback gains tuning is a cornerstone problem of this approach. Different suggestions were made for the gains selection procedure [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. An importance of restrictions on the satellite rotation rate [11], inertia tensor uncertainty [12], and overall control torque value [13] is well established. An inherent problem of the simple feedback law is its inability to provide efficient local disturbances rejection. This results in a low stabilization accuracy which is expected and flight-proven [14] to be around 10–20°. Although very low, this accuracy may be enough for a solar panels illumination, wide field of view antenna pointing, simple Earth remote sensing tasks, and different control system failure situations when low accuracy is the only alternative to the complete mission loss. Expanding magnetic control system capabilities to typical tasks requires at least few degrees accuracy which can be achieved by relatively complex control approaches. Sliding control was proposed to alleviate the control direction restriction by providing proper sliding surface and, hence, the satellite trajectory [[15], [16], [17]]. While paper [15] only proposes the local method of proper sliding surface construction [16,17], provides a cost function that directly includes the projection of the control torque on the geomagnetic induction vector direction. This introduces an important flexibility that local methods lack. Present paper essentially adopts the same general idea, but uses direct reference trajectory construction instead of sliding surface approach. Another approach that utilizes the geomagnetic induction vector, and hence the restricted direction, rotation is based on the model predictive control [18,19]. More detailed survey of the developments in the three axis magnetic control is provided in Refs. [20,21].
Present paper proposes a specific attitude trajectory construction procedure elaborating on the facts of the induction vector rotation and low stabilization accuracy in the orbital reference frame. The latter fact is inherently connected to the restriction in the control torque direction. The satellite encounters relatively well-defined disturbances when stabilized in the orbital reference frame. The disturbing torque that is aligned along the geomagnetic induction vector cannot be suppressed thus leading to inevitably low stabilization accuracy. Instead of the stabilization in the orbital reference frame exactly, the satellite may be stabilized on a trajectory defined by a relatively small attitude angles. This trajectory, just as the exact orbital stabilization, has specific induction vector and disturbances directions associated with every set of attitude angles and time. Maintaining this trajectory, including the disturbances rejection, requires some specific control that is also defined for given attitude and time. Carefully selected trajectory is hopefully characterized by a relatively small control torque along the induction vector. This trajectory can therefore be maintained by the magnetorquers with high accuracy. In the end the satellite is stabilized close to the orbital frame with good (few degrees in total) accuracy instead of the orbital stabilization with low (10–20°) accuracy.
The construction of the above-mentioned trajectory and control is performed with the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) method [[22], [23], [24]] in a two stage procedure. First, the satellite trajectory is constructed. This trajectory is periodic with the double orbital rate. Periodicity of the satellite motion eliminates the necessity of regular trajectory update which is associated with considerable computation effort. Double orbital period complies with the approximate geomagnetic induction vector motion. The satellite trajectory and disturbance torque models define the required control torque. PSO routine is established to search for the trajectory and corresponding torque that has small component along the induction vector. Second stage of the control construction derives the magnetorquers dipole moment from the reference control torque. The control is assigned with the classic feedback structure. Another PSO instance searches for the gains that comply with the restriction on the control torque direction. The final control torque is analyzed for the asymptotic stability. A numerical simulation example illustrates the control capability in the rejection of unknown disturbances.

Conclusions
The paper proposes a method for constructing an angular trajectory, which is realizable by the magnetic attitude control system. The PSO method is applied to find the trajectory with the minimum control torque projection on the geomagnetic induction vector. This reference trajectory is close to the stabilization in the orbital reference frame. Another PSO routine optimizes control gains for the Lyapunov-based control. Introduction
A variety of probes are sent to explore Mars and make endeavours to search the origin of life [1], [2], [3]. In recent years, human beings mainly adopted the rover to detect life on Mars [4], [5], [6], [7]. However, the rover’s vision is not enough to observe some sandpits, preventing the rover from planning the fastest and safest path to its destination [8]. The rugged terrain on Mars has an impact on the movement of the rover, making the rover move slowly or even stuck [9], [10]. Flying in the Mars atmosphere, a small Mars aircraft can expand the rover’s field of view [11]. It will observe more of the surrounding terrain by its camera aerially, and share the images with the rover. After receiving the information, the rover can plan the route better. When there are sandpits and cliffs that the rover cannot bypass, the Mars aircraft can also quickly collect a few soil samples within a distance. Maria Smirnova has proposed a concept vehicle that uses magnesium or aluminum burning in 
CO
2
 to obtain propulsion power, but it is difficult to take such a vehicle to Mars due to current technological limitations [12]. Among the many types of aircraft, rotorcraft is more suitable to complete the above tasks [13]. It can take off and land vertically at any point without a runway, not affected by the Martian terrain [14], [15]. It can also use the wind on Mars to blow the blades to recharge the batteries [16]. Besides, when hovering, rotorcraft is also relatively stable [17], [18]. Grip et al. show that rotorcraft can take special missions on Mars [19], [20].
When the rotor rotates, blades interact with air to generate aerodynamic force that provides lift for the rotorcraft [18]. The results of early Mars exploration show that the air pressure at Martian surface is only 1% of that at Earth’s surface, leading to the low Reynolds number atmospheric environment [21]. Besides, the sound speed of Mars is 72% of that of the earth because of the low average temperature, causing the high Mach number flow field [22], [23], [24], [25]. This extreme environment reduces the mechanical efficiency of the rotor and increases the power consumed by the rotor [26]. Considering that the blade structure determines the aerodynamic performance of the blade itself, it is necessary to design an improved blade structure for the Mars rotorcraft to reduce the power consumption during the Mars exploration mission.
The structural characteristics of the blade include airfoil, twist, and planform (also can be seen as blade chord length distribution). To calculate the aerodynamic performance of blades with different structural characteristics for analysis, simulation is an effective method. A simulation method that uses computational fluid dynamics (CFD) can predict the generated thrust and consumed power of the rotor blade without manufacturing the rotors with different structures [27], [28]. In the past period, the suitable blade airfoil of the Mars rotorcraft has been studied through the two-dimensional CFD simulation and algorithms [29]. Koning et al. used Blade Element Momentum Theory (BEMT) combined with an iterative algorithm to optimize the Mars rotorcraft airfoil [30], [31], [32]. Bohorquez [33], Anyoji [34] and Zhao [35] used a combination of two-dimensional simulation and experimental verification to design the optimal Mars rotorcraft airfoil in different flight conditions and blade diameters. Uwatoko et al. combined Genetic Algorithm (GA) with the two-dimensional simulation method to optimize the airfoil and planform in the Martian environment [27]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are few studies on the blade optimization design method of the Mars rotorcraft blade planform and the twist. In addition, the hovering performance of the Mars rotorcraft blade needs to be calculated by the more convenient simulation method.
In this study, a standard geometrical configuration of Mars rotorcraft blade is defined based on BEMT. A reliable three-dimensional simulation method is adopted combining with the genetic algorithm and neural network to optimize the blade structure parameters suitable for the Mars rotorcraft. The CFD simulation and experimental results clarify that the optimized Mars rotorcraft blade, with a hyperbolic chord length distribution and a specific twist angle, consumes much less power than the conventional blade. In addition, the flow field of the optimized blade structure is discussed to explain its grand performance in the Martian atmosphere.

Abstract

As high-power and high-specific-impulse Hall thrusters develop, the disadvantages of this conventional topology—in terms of material and mechanical strength, processing, and manufacturing—are gradually being exposed. Therefore, in this study, a new wall material was employed, and its effects were investigated numerically and experimentally. Introduction
Electric propulsion has the advantages of a high specific impulse, high efficiency, and high payload ratio compared with conventional chemical propulsion. Thus, it is gradually replacing chemical propulsion in spacecraft in orbit missions, and the application of electric propulsion has become a key index for measuring the performance of satellite platforms [1]. A Hall thruster is an electric propulsion device that uses magnetic confinement to ionize gas and an electrostatic field to accelerate ions. It has the advantages of a simple structure, long service life, and high reliability compared to other types of electric propulsion devices [[2], [3], [4]]. Therefore, it has received significant attention from the scientific community. At present, medium- and low-power Hall thrusters have been widely studied and applied in altitude control, position keeping, and other missions that require low thrust. In the future, Hall thrusters will be used in deep space exploration and long-distance cargo transportation missions because of their good economy and high thrust density, which promotes the development of high-power and high-specific-impulse Hall thrusters [5,6].
A conventional Hall thruster has an annular discharge channel formed by an insulated wall made of a boron nitride (BN) ceramic; the anode is located at the bottom of the channel. The insulated wall covers the high-potential anode for electrical isolation and protects the magnetic circuit from high-energy plasma. The interaction between the insulated wall and electrons produces secondary electrons, which not only “cool” the bulk electrons but also participate in cross-field transport. Therefore, the secondary electron emission (SEE) of the wall materials has a significant impact on propellant ionization and acceleration processes. Theoretical and experimental studies have shown that BN ceramics can optimize the performance of Hall thrusters, owing to their suitable SEE characteristics [7]. However, BN ceramics have certain disadvantages. First, their mechanical properties are poor; because the structure of the channel wall is a U-shaped cantilever, the BN ceramic must be sufficiently thick to ensure sufficient structural strength to overcome the strong vibration generated in the satellite launch stage. This would make a channel wall, used in high-power and high-specific-impulse Hall thrusters, cumbersome. Additionally, the radial space occupied by the wall poses challenges in designing an efficient magnetic circuit, and the coil power loss is considerably increased. Second, the wall surface was coated with conducting film during the ground test. A nonuniform coating causes a difference in the local wall conductivity. Consequently, high voltage micro-discharge occurs near the anode, where the local conductivity is large, which can cause the thruster to shut down in certain situations and delay the test process. Third, limited by the molding equipment and processing, it is difficult to fabricate an entire BN channel wall of a large size, which is required for high-power and high-specific-impulse Hall thrusters. These shortcomings of BN ceramics impede their future application in Hall thrusters. Therefore, new wall materials or design schemes are required to solve these problems and maintain the competitive advantage of Hall thrusters in space-propulsion missions.
Research on replacing the wall materials of Hall thrusters was conducted as early as the 1990s, and comprehensive results were obtained around the year 2000. Tahara et al. [7] demonstrated that the wall material has a significant influence on the distribution of the ionization region under different magnetic field intensities and discharge voltages. The ionization region is closer to the acceleration region because of the high SEE coefficient of the BN ceramic, and the generated ions can be effectively accelerated to obtain the highest operation efficiency. Gascon et al. [8] conducted a comparative study on the discharge characteristics of an SPT-100 Hall thruster by replacing the BN ceramic with graphite, Al2O3, and SiC. They found that the discharge current (mainly electronic current) and oscillation increased significantly after the replacement, and the maximum difference in efficiency was as high as 20% relative to that of the BN ceramic. Additionally, studies on the partial replacement of low-emission electrode materials have been conducted. The simulation results of Qing et al. [9,10] showed that when a low-emission graphite electrode is placed near the ionization region, and a positive bias voltage higher than the discharge voltage is applied, closed potential contour pockets (PCPs) can be created inside the channel. These PCPs can effectively reduce plume divergence. However, adding a high-voltage electrode incurs additional power consumption, resulting in an increase in the electronic current, which is not conducive to improving thruster efficiency. The substantial decline in performance has led to a temporary suspension of research on the replacement of wall materials and segmented electrodes.
Relevant research has restarted since long-lifetime technologies such as “magnetic shielding” and “aft-loading magnetic field” were proposed around 2010. The main feature is that the acceleration and partial ionization regions are pushed outside the thruster channel; consequently, the electron temperature inside the channel significantly decreases. As secondary electrons are only generated when the incident electron temperature exceeds a certain threshold, the influence of the SEE characteristics of the wall materials on the discharge, is reduced. Therefore, research on replacing the wall materials has become popular. Grimaud et al. [11] confirmed that the decrease in the electron temperature inside the channel, caused by the change in magnetic field characteristics, is the main reason for the decrease in the dependence of the performance on the properties of wall materials in magnetically shielded Hall thrusters. Goebel et al. [12] compared the discharge performance of graphite and BN walls on a 6 kW H6MS Hall thruster with a magnetically shielded design. The experimental results showed that the efficiency and thrust decreased slightly, by 1%, with a graphite wall. On a 200 W Hall thruster with an aft-loaded magnetic field, Ding et al. [13,14] found that the performance of a titanium wall is slightly lower than that of a BN ceramic wall, and the maximum difference in efficiency is approximately 3%.
Magnetic shielding and aft-loading technologies can significantly decrease the interaction between the plasma and the wall, greatly reducing the sensitivity of the discharge process to the SEE characteristics of the wall materials. However, the performance of all the other wall materials did not exceed that of the BN ceramic. This indicates that the BN ceramic is still the best wall material for the strong discharge area of Hall thrusters, from the perspective of performance benefits.
In most of the aforementioned studies, the channel wall adopted a single material. However, the requirement for the SEE characteristics of the wall material varies in different discharge regions. The SEE from the wall is not necessary to cool the bulk electrons upstream of the channel, where the electron temperature is relatively low. Nevertheless, the high electron temperature area downstream of the channel requires a high SEE to control the electron temperature and improve ionization. A single material cannot meet all the SEE requirements along the entire discharge channel. Research on the segmented electrode wall showed that it regulates the ion beam current, but additional power is lost on the segmented electrode [9,10]. Thus, the overall efficiency may be reduced. The thruster structure also becomes more complex owing to the additional power supply connection and necessary electrode insulation. Moreover, it becomes more difficult for thrusters with segmented electrodes to control the enhanced electron cross-field transport at high discharge voltages, and thus is not favorable for the realization of a high specific impulse.
In this study, to overcome the disadvantages of the BN ceramic as the only material of the channel wall, and satisfy the different SEE requirements at different discharge regions, a hybrid wall structure is proposed. The BN ceramic wall was retained downstream of the channel, where the discharge was strong and the electron temperature was high, whereas a metallic wall was used upstream of the channel. As for the magnetic field, it was verified in our previous work that the aft-loaded magnetic field is suitable and efficient for high-voltage operations [15]. The application of an aft-loaded magnetic field also results in a long thruster lifetime and low sensitivity to the wall material. Therefore, to promote the development of high-power and high-specific-impulse Hall thrusters, a hybrid-wall Hall thruster with an aft-loaded magnetic field is adopted, and the influence of the length of the metallic wall on the high-voltage discharge characteristics is studied numerically and experimentally.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, the influence of a hybrid wall on the discharge performance of a Hall thruster, with an aft-loaded magnetic field, is simulated and a qualitative understanding is established. Section 3 presents the measured high-voltage discharge characteristics of the hybrid-wall Hall thruster. In Section 4, the physics involved in the numerical and experimental phenomena are analyzed and discussed. Finally, the conclusions are presented in Section 5.

Abstract
The problem of attitude stabilization of a small satellite using magnetic actuators is considered, a review of the existing approaches based on linear and nonlinear control theory (with particular emphasis on periodic control) is proposed and a solution to the problem in terms of model-based predictive control is presented and analyzed. Simulation results are also given, which show the feasibility of the predictive approach.
Introduction
Attitude control systems (ACS) play a fundamental role in the operation of spacecraft as they constitutes a mandatory feature both for the survival of a satellite and for the satisfactory achievement of mission goals. While a number of possible approaches to the control of attitude dynamics has been developed through the years, a particularly effective and reliable one is constituted by the use of electromagnetic actuators, which turn out to be specially suitable in practice for low Earth orbit (LEO) satellites. Such actuators operate on the basis of the interaction between a set of three orthogonal, current-driven magnetic coils and the magnetic field of the Earth (Wertz, 1978; Sidi, 1997) and therefore provide a very simple solution to the problem of generating torques on board a satellite. The major drawback of this control technique is that the torques which can be applied to the spacecraft for attitude control purposes are constrained to lie in the plane orthogonal to the magnetic field vector. In particular, three axis magnetic stabilization is only possible if the considered orbit “sees” a variation of the magnetic field which is sufficient to guarantee the stabilizability of the spacecraft (see Bhat & Dham, 2003).
The use of magnetic coils for control purposes has been the subject of extensive study since the early years of satellite missions (see White, Shigemoto, & Bourquin, 1961), both for attitude control and for momentum management on spacecraft controlled with reaction wheels (see Camillo & Markley, 1980) for an overview of magnetic momentum dumping). Until recent years, however, only approximate solutions to the problem of dealing with such time-varying actuators were available (see, e.g., Stickler & Alfriend, 1976; Arduini & Baiocco, 1997). In particular, while periodic control has been already proved successful in various applications (see, e.g., Arcara, Bittanti, & Lovera, 2000), the feasibility of periodic techniques for the control of small satellites using magnetic actuators has become only recently a topic of active research (see, e.g., the recent works (Pittelkau, 1993; De Marchi, Rocco, Morea, & Lovera, 1999; Wisniewski and Blanke, 1999; Wisniewski and Markley, 1999, Wang and Shtessel, 1999; Lovera, De Marchi, & Bittanti, 2002).
In the recent literature, both the linear and the nonlinear attitude control problems have been investigated; the former has been studied mostly in an LQ perspective, while the latter has been investigated using Lyapunov methods and exploiting periodicity of the Earth magnetic field in order to apply Krasovski–La Salle Theorem for the stability analysis. No actual periodic nonlinear controllers have been developed so far, to the best knowledge of the authors. In the light of the above considerations, the aim of this paper is twofold. First of all to present a detailed survey of the (numerous) approaches to the analysis and design of magnetic attitude control systems which have been proposed in the last 10–15 years. In addition, a novel approach to the magnetic attitude control problem, based on a predictive approach is proposed in this paper. The novel idea is to consider the magnetically controlled spacecraft as a time invariant system and to incorporate the time-varying actuators in the problem formulation via an appropriate set of constraints. It is possible to show that the state feedback problem can be given a simple closed-form solution, and that actuator saturation constraints can also be dealt with in the same framework. The proposed control design methodology has been tested in a simulation study for a spacecraft of the MITA class (see Della Torre, Lupi, Sabatini, & Coppola, 1999) and the (satisfactory) results obtained so far have been also reported.
The paper is organized as follows: in Section 2, a description of the dynamics of a spacecraft will be presented, and a linearized dynamic model derived; 3 Linear design methods, 4 Nonlinear design methods offer an overview of the relevant results in linear and nonlinear control which are available in the literature for this problem; Section 5 presents the (novel) predictive approach to the magnetic attitude control problems, while in Section 6 the results obtained with the predictive magnetic controller in a simulation study are shown.

Magnetic predictive attitude control
The aim of this section is to propose a novel approach to the magnetic attitude control problem based on the application of predictive control ideas. Precisely, the magnetically controlled spacecraft is modelled as a time-invariant system with three independent control inputs, and the presence of magnetic actuators is taken into account via a suitable set of constraints.

Concluding remarks
The attitude control problem for a spacecraft using magnetic actuators has been considered and analyzed in the framework of periodic control theory. A set of solutions in terms of classical LQ and H∞ periodic control and nonlinear control have been discussed and extensions thereof, aiming at achieving robustness of the control law with respect to uncertain parameters have been proposed.
