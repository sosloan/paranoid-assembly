Ada Lovelace looked across the winter Thames and did not see a machine
that could merely calculate. She saw an engine able to accept symbols,
abstract their patterns, and return a new kind of thought.

With Charles Babbage, she began a patient task: to declare each wheel,
column, and card as part of a larger design. Her notes were a package
of ideas, private at first but meant for all. A number was not only a
constant; it could be a record of a melody, a map, or a calculation.
She imagined an array of such records, indexed over a range and altered
by procedure rather than accident. The delta between two figures was
not a mystery; it was a difference the Engine could name.

If the Engine received a question, then it could select a method. Case
by case, it might add, subtract, or multiply; elsif the answer required
another card, it could delay its work; else it could proceed. Its
instructions might say, "do
this," yet a careful pragma would make clear that no shortcut was
overriding the design. For each operation, a loop would
begin, continue while its condition held, and exit when the work was
done. Ada knew that an exception must not become an excuse for chaos:
when a result was null, the operator should raise a signal, not hide it.

Her celebrated plan for Bernoulli numbers was a function in spirit,
though the Engine had no body to run it. It would use variables with
digits, carry a remainder by rem or mod, and reverse a sequence when
the cards required it. Some values would be in, others out; access to
them would be limited, aliased only with care, and never trusted merely
because they were new.

She described the Engine as a general instrument. It could be generic
enough to serve astronomy or music, yet a subtype could keep each
quantity within its proper bounds. A type might be tagged, an interface
might be synchronized, and a protected task might wait at an entry.
Such order, she believed, would let separate parts work together without
confusing one another.

Ada would not abort an inquiry because its first path failed. She would
requeue the question, use another method, or goto the evidence and
begin again. She renames no uncertainty as certainty, nor would she
terminate a question before its proof is complete. Not every insight is
immediate: abs alone did not explain
the negative, xor did not settle a choice, and neither and nor or could
replace imagination. Yet she held that a machine could follow rules
until it reached the end of a calculation—and that people could use
those rules to discover what had not been seen before.

In that vision, every instruction had a place: at the right moment,
with the right value, for the right purpose. The Analytical Engine
would be an instrument of reason; Ada Lovelace made it, in language,
an instrument of possibility.
